The Long Road of Blue

Curated by ruminate18

Blue is rare in nature and hard to make. For most of history, anyone who wanted it had to cook it from copper and sand, dig it out of a few distant mines, or buy it from someone who had. These six objects show how people made blue and how it moved between workshops across Asia, mostly as cobalt mined in Iran, carried to China, and sent back west as Chinese design.

Where the works fall, to scale
  1. 8th–7th century BCE1 of 6 · Fragments

    Fragment of Egyptian blue on mud from the Palace of Sargon at Khorsabad

    Assyrian, Neo-Assyrian period8th–7th century BCEEgyptian blue pigment applied to mud plaster

    This lump of mud once belonged to a wall at Khorsabad, the capital Sargon II built in what is now northern Iraq. Painters gave the mud a white lime coat, then laid on Egyptian blue, a pigment made by heating sand, lime and copper together. Egyptians invented it more than four thousand years ago, and it is the oldest known synthetic pigment. Rutherford Gettens, one of Harvard's first conservation scientists, studied this sample.

    Why it's here

    It is where the story starts: blue as a manufactured color, already traveling far from Egypt, where it was invented. Nothing else in the show is this plain or this old.

    Gift of Prentice Duell1942.219.1
  2. first half 8th century2 of 6 · Vessels

    Jar with cobalt-blue splashes

    Chinese, Tang dynasty, 618-907first half 8th centuryWhite earthenware with leadfluxed, cobaltsplashed glazeH. 18.1 x Diam. 21 cm (7 1/8 x 8 1/4 in.)

    Tang potters in Henan usually decorated this kind of ware with amber, green and cream glazes. Here they used cobalt blue instead, letting it run down the shoulders. Cobalt was costly in Tang China and was probably imported from western Asia, so blue pieces like this one are much rarer than the other colors. A lab test of clay from the base dates the jar to the Tang period.

    Why it's here

    This is cobalt's first stop in the show. The blue is still loose and accidental. Six centuries later, the same mineral would become the most controlled brushwork in Chinese ceramics.

    Partial gift of the Walter C. Sedgwick Foundation and partial purchase through the Ernest B. and Helen Pratt Dane Fund for Asian Art2006.170.264
  3. 12th century3 of 6 · Vessels

    Cup with Lobed Rim and Human Faces

    Seljuk-Atabeg period12th centuryFritware with molded relief decoration under blue (cobalt) transparent alkali glaze10.8 x 14.2 cm (4 1/4 x 5 9/16 in.)

    A ring of almond-eyed faces is pressed into the walls of this cup, and the rim swells to follow them. The whole thing sits under one coat of transparent cobalt glaze, which pooled and ran onto the base during firing. On one side the glaze has broken down and turned iridescent. The body is fritware, a ground-quartz paste that Iranian potters developed in this period to get closer to the whiteness of Chinese porcelain.

    Why it's here

    It shows cobalt in its homeland, in Iran, where the mineral was mined. Here blue covers the whole surface. In the next two works it becomes a painted line.

    The Norma Jean Calderwood Collection of Islamic Art2002.50.95
  4. Ming dynasty, perhaps Tianshun period (1457-1464)4 of 6 · Vessels

    Meiping Bottle with a Daoist Immortal and His Youthful Attendant in a Windswept Landscape

    Chinese, Ming dynasty, 1368-1644Ming dynasty, perhaps Tianshun period (1457-1464)Blue-and-white ware: porcelain with decoration painted in underglaze cobalt blueH. 33.4 cm (13 1/8 in.)

    The bottle is painted in three bands. Cresting waves run along the base, lotus petals hang from the shoulder, and in the middle an immortal and a young attendant lean into the wind. The painter worked in cobalt on unfired porcelain and then covered it with clear glaze, so the blue sits under the surface and does not wear off. Ming potters at Jingdezhen made this technique, called underglaze blue, famous across the world.

    Why it's here

    This is the high point of the cobalt story: a mineral from Iran turned into fine brushwork on Chinese porcelain. Compare it with the dish next to it, made in Iran about fifteen years later.

    Purchase through the generosity of Alan J. and Suzanne W. Dworsky, Dorothy Tapper Goldman, Christina Marcove, Alan L. and Jacqueline B. Stuart and through the Louise Haskell Daly Fund2012.195
  5. c.14755 of 6 · Vessels

    Dish with Peonies

    Persian, Timurid periodc.1475Fritware painted with blue (cobalt) under clear alkali glaze7.8 x 43 cm (3 1/16 x 16 15/16 in.)

    Chinese blue-and-white porcelain was collected eagerly in Iran, and Iranian potters set out to copy it. This dish borrows from two Chinese periods: the wave border comes from Yuan wares and the fat peonies from Ming ones. The potter did not have porcelain clay, so the dish is made of fritware. The flowers crowd the center a little unevenly, which suggests a painter adapting a foreign pattern by eye.

    Why it's here

    It closes the loop. Cobalt went east from Iran to China, and here the Chinese way of using it comes back west to Iran. Seen together, the bottle and this dish show two cultures sharing a single blue.

    The Norma Jean Calderwood Collection of Islamic Art2002.50.66
  6. Heisei period, 20086 of 6 · Sculpture

    Windy Seascape (Kaze no kaikei)

    Fukami Sueharu 深見陶治, JapaneseHeisei period, 2008Molded porcelain with pale, sky-blue glaze (Chinese qingbai-type glaze); with signature reading "S. Fukami"; numbered 2/5H. 32.0 cm x W. 165.5 cm x D. 27.0 cm (12 5/8 x 65 3/16 x 10 5/8 in.)

    More than a meter and a half long, this porcelain blade dips in the middle and narrows to two razor-thin points, like a wave caught by the wind. Fukami molds porcelain under pressure to get edges this sharp, then covers it in qingbai, a pale glaze first used in Song-dynasty China. The faint blue does not come from cobalt. It comes from traces of iron in the glaze, fired in a kiln starved of oxygen.

    Why it's here

    The show ends with a blue that needs no imported mineral and no painter. It is a quiet answer to everything before it, and it makes the point that blue can come from the firing as much as from the pigment.

    Ernest B. and Helen Pratt Dane Fund for Asian Art and through the generosity of William B. Goldstein, M.D.2008.243
